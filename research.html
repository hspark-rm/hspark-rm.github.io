My research asks a single question in three settings: how does disadvantage accumulate within a person, within a family, and across generations — and when is it reversible?

Discrimination and immigrant well-being

Discrimination is usually measured once and treated as a stable trait. It is neither. People move into and out of discriminatory environments, and the consequences of entering may not mirror the benefits of leaving.

I follow immigrant women and their children in South Korea over multiple waves to separate the effect of becoming discriminated against from the effect of that experience ending. This asymmetry matters for policy: if the harm of onset is not undone by its cessation, prevention and remediation are not interchangeable.

Precarious employment and the labour market

Employment insecurity is a moving state, not a category. Using asymmetric fixed effects models, I show that transitions into precarious employment and transitions out of it carry unequal weight for well-being — losses are not simply gains reversed.

Related work examines how early disadvantage — adolescent depression, juvenile justice contact — channels people into insecure work in adulthood, using sibling comparisons to hold family background constant.

Life course and intergenerational transmission

My dissertation traced discrimination across two generations within the same families: how immigrant mothers’ experiences of foreign-origin discrimination relate to their children’s bullying victimization, mental health, and life satisfaction, and what buffers that transmission.

Methods

I work primarily with longitudinal panel data and within-unit identification strategies:

  • Fixed effects (FE) — within-person change, absorbing time-invariant confounders
  • Asymmetric fixed effects (AFE) — separating the effect of increases from decreases in exposure (Allison 2019)
  • Sibling fixed effects — within-family identification
  • Mediation and moderation analysis, multiple imputation, inverse probability weighting

I report pooled OLS, random effects, fixed effects, and asymmetric fixed effects estimates side by side, so that the contribution of each identifying assumption is visible rather than assumed. Analyses are implemented in Stata and R.
